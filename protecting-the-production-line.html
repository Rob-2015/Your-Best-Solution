Manufacturing by the numbers, 2026

27.7% of all attacks

Manufacturing took more cyber attacks than any other sector last year, for the fifth year running (IBM X-Force 2026).

61% ransomware

Of manufacturing breaches involved ransomware, and malware of some kind was in 75% (Verizon DBIR 2026).

61% third party

Of manufacturing breaches involved a supplier, vendor or partner (Verizon DBIR 2026).

£3m per UK breach

The average cost of a UK breach, with about 225 days to find and contain it (IBM UK Cost of a Data Breach 2026). The worldwide average for industrial businesses is £4m.

87% about money

Of attacks are financially motivated, and a further 15% are espionage, after your designs (Verizon DBIR 2026).

43% of UK businesses

Were hit by a breach or attack in the last year, rising to 69% of large firms (UK Cyber Breaches Survey 2025/26).

1,589 attempts a week

The number of attack attempts per UK organisation every week, up 34% on a year ago (Check Point, June 2026).

Only 31% at board level

Of UK businesses have someone at board level responsible for cyber, and only 25% have a written incident response plan.

The five threats that matter most

Ransomware

Involved in 61% of manufacturing breaches. Your systems are locked and your data stolen until you pay, or rebuild.

Unpatched systems

38% of breaches started with an exploited weakness in kit facing the internet. It's now the number one way in.

Supply chain attacks

61% of breaches involved a third party. Someone else's weak spot becomes your problem, and yours becomes your customer's.

Phone and text scams

40% more likely to work than an email. The fake call from IT asking for a code is the scam to watch.

Intellectual property leaking through AI

45% of staff now use AI regularly at work, 67% of them through personal accounts. Drawings, specs and source code are being pasted into tools you don't control.

What it really costs

Jaguar Land Rover, UK, 2025

Five weeks with no car production, an estimated £1.9 billion cost to the UK economy and more than 5,000 organisations affected (Cyber Monitoring Centre).

Asahi Group, Japan, late 2025

Ransomware closed its domestic factories, suspended shipments and put corporate data at risk.

Kreisel GmbH, Germany, 2024

Ransomware halted operations for weeks. It refused to pay, never recovered and filed for insolvency that November.

Why that matters to you

JLR had the size and the balance sheet to ride it out. Kreisel didn't. Most of the manufacturers I talk to in Essex, Kent and London look a lot more like Kreisel than JLR. Does that make sense?

The fundamentals checklist

1. Patch fast

Patch anything facing the internet within 14 days of a critical fix, and know who owns that job.

2. Multi-factor authentication

Turn it on for email, remote access and every cloud system.

3. Clean up accounts

Remove old accounts and limit admin rights to the few people who need them.

4. Backups you can trust

Keep offline or immutable backups, and test a restore at least twice a year.

5. Separate production

Separate your production network (OT) from the office network. OT and industrial cybersecurity

6. Brief your staff

Warn them about phone and text scams, with a simple call back to check rule.

7. One-page AI policy

Write it down and name the tools people are allowed to use.

8. Check suppliers

Ask key suppliers about their cyber standards and put a minimum in the contract.

9. Plan the first hour

Write down who does what in the first hour of an attack, including who rings the insurer.

10. Get certified

Get Cyber Essentials, then decide whether buyers need Plus or ISO 27001. Do I need Cyber Essentials?

Common questions about manufacturing cyber risk

How do attackers get into manufacturers?

The most common way in is now an unpatched system facing the internet, at 38% of manufacturing breaches, ahead of phishing at 13% and stolen logins at 11% (Verizon DBIR 2026). Stolen credentials featured in 41% of manufacturing breaches overall.

What is the first thing a manufacturer should fix?

Patch anything facing the internet and turn on multi-factor authentication for email, remote access and every cloud system. None of this needs a genius hacker. It needs a gap that nobody has got round to closing, and gaps can be closed.

Should we ban AI tools to protect our designs?

No. Pick approved tools, write a one-page policy and tell people what they can and can't paste in. Verizon found 3.2% of uploads were research and technical documentation, so expect buyers to start asking about it.

Does Cyber Essentials reduce insurance claims?

The UK Government says organisations with Cyber Essentials are 92% less likely to make a claim on their cyber insurance.
